#### **Flower Sweep**

- **Setup:**
    - Control one arm and grip their pants or belt on the same side.
    - Open your guard and pivot your hips, using one leg to swing out wide and the other to sweep their base.
- **Key Detail:**
    - Use momentum from your swinging leg to destabilize their posture and drive the sweep.

#### **Counter Sweep**

- **Setup:**
    - Wait for your opponent to commit to a pass or attempt to shift their weight.
    - Use their momentum against them by controlling their arm or leg and executing a sweep in the opposite direction of their pressure.
- **Key Detail:**
    - Timing is critical—capitalize on their movement to reverse their position effectively.

#### **Pendulum Sweep**

- **Setup:**
    - Control their arm on one side and grip their pants or belt on the other.
    - Open your guard and swing your far leg like a pendulum while using the other leg to block their base.
- **Key Detail:**
    - Generate momentum with a strong pendulum motion, ensuring your grips remain tight to maintain control throughout the sweep.
#### **Offensive Sweep**

- **Setup:**
    - Initiate proactively by creating angles and disrupting their base through grips and hip movement.
    - Use setups like butterfly guard hooks, collar grips, or underhooks to force a reaction.
- **Key Detail:**
    - Aggression and timing are essential—drive the sweep with purpose and anticipate counters to maintain control.

### Framing and Establishing Points of Contact in BJJ

- **Far Arm for Framing**:
    
    - Use your far arm (opposite to the shoulder that's on the ground) to initiate framing. This is crucial for creating initial space and controlling the opponent's movement.
- **Near Arm for Contact**:
    
    - The near arm (same side as the shoulder on the ground) is used to establish a strong point of contact with the opponent. This arm works to maintain control and reinforce positioning after the frame is set.
- **Legs for Flexibility**:
    
    - If your legs are engaged with your opponent (e.g., hooking or controlling their hips), you can afford to be more dynamic with how you use the far arm. This allows you to shift focus from strictly framing to exploring offensive opportunities, such as sweeps or submissions.
- **Integrating Movements**:
    
    - Start with the far arm to create space, then bring the near arm into play to maintain contact and control. Transition the forearm of the near arm to establish additional points of contact.
- **Advancing Position**:
    
    - Use the control gained from framing and contact to gradually manipulate both of the opponent’s arms. Once you achieve control, you can work toward sweeps, positional improvements, or submissions.

This method combines both arms effectively while leveraging your legs to stay flexible and adapt to changing opportunities.


Submission counters
  
  
1. **Armbar (Juji Gatame)**  
- **Counter Submission:**  
- **Leg Lock**: As the opponent extends for the armbar, you can use your legs to isolate one of their legs for a counter leg lock.  
- **Triangle Choke**: If you manage to pull your arm out and they remain in a compromised position.  
  
2. **Triangle Choke (Sankaku Jime)**  
- **Counter Submission:**  
- **Ankle Lock**: If you can posture up and isolate their legs.  
- **Kneebar**: By transitioning your hips under their legs as you posture up.  
  
3. **Omoplata**  
- **Counter Submission:**  
- **Straight Ankle Lock**: As they attempt the omoplata, you can attack their leg.  
- **Toe Hold**: If you can isolate their foot while they roll to complete the omoplata.  
  
4. **Kimura**  
- **Counter Submission:**  
- **Straight Armbar**: If you can turn your arm and grip their arm while they attempt the kimura.  
- **Triangle Choke**: If you can isolate their head and arm by using your legs.  
  
5. **Guillotine Choke**  
- **Counter Submission:**  
- **Von Flue Choke**: If you pass to the side control while they hold the guillotine.  
- **Arm Triangle Choke**: If you can control their arm and head from the top position.  
  
6. **Rear Naked Choke (RNC)**  
- **Counter Submission:**  
- **Straight Armbar**: If you can control their arm before they lock the choke.  
- **Triangle Choke**: If you manage to roll and trap their arm with your legs.  
  
7. **Americana**  
- **Counter Submission:**  
- **Kimura**: By reversing the grip and attacking their arm.  
- **Triangle Choke**: If you manage to trap their arm and head with your legs.  
  
8. **Bow and Arrow Choke**  
- **Counter Submission:**  
- **Leg Lock**: As they extend to finish the choke, you can attack their leg.  
- **Reverse Armbar**: If you manage to control their extended arm.  
  
9. **Leg Lock (e.g., Straight Ankle Lock)**  
- **Counter Submission:**  
- **Heel Hook**: By isolating their leg as they attack yours.  
- **Toe Hold**: If you can control their foot during their leg lock attempt.  
  
10. **Darce Choke**  
- **Counter Submission:**  
- **Anaconda Choke**: By reversing the position and attacking their neck.  
- **Guillotine Choke**: If you manage to control their head and neck during their transition.


Clearing full guard
1. **Posture and Base Establishment**:  
- **Posture Up**: Maintain a strong posture by sitting back slightly and keeping your back straight. This makes it harder for your opponent to break your posture and attack.  
- **Grip Control**: Control your opponent’s hips or sleeves to prevent them from executing sweeps or submissions.  
  
2. **Knee in Tailbone (Knee Cutter Pass)**:  
- **Knee in Center**: Position one knee in the centerline of your opponent’s hips, driving it into their tailbone. This helps to open their guard.  
- **Foot Positioning**: Post one foot up on the mat to create leverage.  
- **Breaking the Guard**: Push your opponent’s knee down with your hand or forearm while using your knee to apply pressure on their crossed ankles, encouraging them to open their guard.  
  
3. **Standing Guard Break**:  
- **Grip Control**: Secure a strong grip on your opponent’s gi pants or belt.  
- **Stand Up**: Place one foot on the mat and then stand up, keeping a wide base for balance.  
- **Guard Opening**: Once standing, push down on your opponent’s knee with one hand while maintaining control of their pants with the other, using your weight to break the guard open.  
  
4. **Double Under Pass**:  
- **Underhook Legs**: Reach both arms under your opponent’s legs and clasp your hands together.  
- **Stack Your Opponent**: Lift their hips and stack them on their shoulders, causing discomfort and often opening their guard.  
- **Pass the Guard**: Move to one side while keeping pressure, transitioning to side control.  
  
5. **Can Opener (Caution: Controversial Technique)**:  
- **Head Control**: Grab the back of your opponent’s head with both hands and pull it towards you.  
- **Apply Pressure**: Apply upward pressure on their neck, which can force them to open their guard to relieve the discomfort.  
- **Transition**: Once their guard opens, move quickly to pass.  
  
6. **Combat Base Guard Break**:  
- **Combat Base**: Slide one knee between your opponent’s legs while keeping the other knee up, creating a combat base stance.  
- **Leverage and Pressure**: Use your elbow and forearm to apply pressure on their thigh while using your knee to pry open the guard.  
  
7. **Hip Control and Leg Pin**:  
- **Control Hips**: Place both hands on your opponent’s hips, pushing them down.  
- **Pin Leg**: Step one leg back and then pin your opponent’s leg with your other leg, breaking their guard.

In Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu, there are several open guards that work effectively when your opponent remains on their knees or in a low posture. These guards allow you to control, create space, and set up sweeps or submissions without relying on your opponent standing. Here are some options:  
  
  
---  
  
1. Butterfly Guard  
  
Overview: Sit up with your legs bent and feet hooking inside your opponent’s thighs. Your hands control their sleeves, wrists, or collars, and your posture is upright or slightly leaning back.  
  
Why it works: Butterfly guard is great for controlling a kneeling opponent by lifting or sweeping them using your hooks and grips. It's versatile for sweeps and transitions into attacks like armlocks or guillotines.  
  
  
  
---  
  
2. Half Guard  
  
Overview: Trap one of your opponent's legs between yours while controlling their upper body with an underhook, overhook, or collar grip.  
  
Why it works: Half guard is a solid position for creating space, setting up sweeps (like the Old School or Deep Half sweeps), or transitioning into submissions like a kimura or back take.  
  
  
  
---  
  
3. Seated Guard (Sit-Up Guard)  
  
Overview: Sit up with your legs in front, feet ready to push against your opponent’s thighs or hips. Use grips on their sleeves, lapels, or collar to control distance.  
  
Why it works: This is a flexible position that allows you to create distance, break grips, and move into single-leg sweeps or transition into other guards.  
  
  
  
---  
  
4. Cross Collar and Sleeve Guard  
  
Overview: Use a cross-collar grip and control one of your opponent’s sleeves while keeping your legs in an open position. Your feet can go on their hips or thighs.  
  
Why it works: The cross-collar grip provides excellent control over their posture, and the sleeve grip helps you set up arm drags, triangles, or sweeps like the scissor sweep.  
  
  
  
---  
  
5. Closed Guard Transitions to Open  
  
Overview: Start in closed guard and transition into an open position by placing feet on the opponent’s hips or gripping their sleeves while maintaining a solid connection with your legs.  
  
Why it works: This gradual transition allows you to maintain control and create space without needing them to stand up. You can move into spider guard, lasso guard, or collar-and-sleeve guard depending on how they react.  
  
  
  
---  
  
6. Collar Tie and Shin-to-Shin Guard  
  
Overview: Control their posture with one hand gripping the collar or head, and place your shin against their shin. Use this to off-balance or transition into a sweep.  
  
Why it works: This guard disrupts their base and allows you to move into stronger positions like single-leg x-guard without them standing.  
  
  
  
---  
  
7. Z Guard (Knee Shield)  
  
Overview: From a half-guard position, place your top shin across their chest or hips while controlling their upper body with an underhook or frame.  
  
Why it works: The knee shield creates space, making it harder for them to pressure you. It's an excellent platform for sweeps, submissions, or transitioning into other guards.  
  
  
  
---  
  
8. Spider Guard on Knees  
  
Overview: While your opponent is kneeling, use grips on their sleeves and place your feet on their biceps. Adjust pressure with your legs to create space or off-balance them.  
  
Why it works: Spider guard can be adapted for kneeling opponents to control their posture and arms, leading to sweeps or submissions.  
  
  
  
---  
  
9. Deep Half Guard  
  
Overview: Get under your opponent’s hips by scooping beneath one of their legs while trapping their thigh with your legs.  
  
Why it works: Deep half guard is great for sweeping or transitioning into leg entanglements while keeping you safe from pressure passes.  
  
  
  
---  
  
10. Scissor Guard  
  
Overview: Use a cross-collar and sleeve grip, one leg across their midsection, and the other leg placed on the ground. Push and pull to create a sweeping motion.  
  
Why it works: This classic guard works well against a kneeling opponent and allows you to maintain control and set up basic sweeps.  
  
  
  
---  
  
Key Tips:  
  
Control Grips: Always prioritize strong grips on the collar, sleeves, or wrists to control their posture.  
  
Stay Active: Use your legs to constantly push, pull, and off-balance your opponent.  
  
Transitions: Be ready to flow between guards to adapt to your opponent’s reactions.  
  
  
By combining these guards, you’ll have a strong arsenal to control and attack opponents who choose to stay low rather than stand up.
