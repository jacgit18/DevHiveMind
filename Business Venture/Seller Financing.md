In the context of buying a business, **seller financing** (also called **owner financing**) is when the seller of the business acts as the lender, allowing the buyer to purchase the business through installment payments rather than securing a traditional loan from a bank or financial institution.

### **How Seller Financing Works in a Business Purchase**

1. **Negotiation of Terms** – The buyer and seller agree on the loan amount, interest rate, down payment, repayment period, and any collateral.
2. **Purchase Agreement & Promissory Note** – A legal contract outlines the terms of the financing, including repayment schedules and default consequences.
3. **Due Diligence & Transition** – The buyer ensures the business’s financials are sound and may negotiate training or transition support from the seller.
4. **Payments to Seller** – The buyer makes agreed-upon payments to the seller over time while operating the business.
5. **Final Ownership Transfer** – Once the loan is fully paid, the seller releases any remaining claims on the business (if applicable).

### **Why Seller Financing is Common in Business Sales**

- **Easier Financing** – Banks may be reluctant to fund small business purchases, so seller financing provides an alternative.
- **Shows Seller Confidence** – If the seller is willing to finance, it signals that they believe in the business’s profitability.
- **Wider Buyer Pool** – Buyers who may not qualify for a traditional loan can still purchase the business.
- **Tax Benefits for Seller** – Spreading out payments over time can reduce the seller’s immediate tax burden.

### **Typical Seller Financing Terms for Businesses**

- Down payment: 10-50% of the purchase price
- Loan term: 3-7 years (sometimes longer)
- Interest rate: 5-10% (varies by market and agreement)
- Collateral: Often includes business assets, personal guarantees, or liens

### **Checklist for Evaluating Seller Financing in a Business Purchase**

#### **1. Assess the Business Itself**

✅ **Financial Statements Review** – Examine at least 3 years of profit/loss statements, balance sheets, and tax returns.  
✅ **Cash Flow Analysis** – Ensure the business generates enough revenue to cover loan payments and operational costs.  
✅ **Valuation** – Get an independent business valuation to ensure the asking price is fair.  
✅ **Industry & Market Conditions** – Assess trends, competition, and potential risks.

#### **2. Evaluate the Seller's Financing Offer**

✅ **Down Payment Requirement** – Determine the percentage required upfront (typically 10-50%).  
✅ **Loan Terms & Interest Rate** – Compare with traditional financing options (common interest rates: 5-10%).  
✅ **Repayment Schedule** – Check if it aligns with expected business cash flow.  
✅ **Balloon Payments** – Identify if a large lump sum is required at the end of the term.  
✅ **Prepayment Terms** – See if early payoff is allowed without penalties.

#### **3. Verify Legal & Contractual Details**

✅ **Promissory Note** – Ensure a legally binding agreement with clear repayment terms.  
✅ **Security & Collateral** – Understand if the seller is taking a lien on business assets or requiring personal guarantees.  
✅ **Non-Compete Agreement** – Ensure the seller won’t open a competing business after the sale.  
✅ **Default & Repossession Terms** – Know the consequences if payments are missed.

#### **4. Conduct Due Diligence on the Seller**

✅ **Verify Business Ownership & Debts** – Ensure there are no undisclosed liabilities or legal disputes.  
✅ **Check UCC Filings** – See if the business has outstanding loans that could affect ownership transfer.  
✅ **Assess Seller’s Motivation** – A motivated seller might offer better terms.

#### **5. Plan for a Smooth Transition**

✅ **Training & Handover Agreement** – Negotiate a transition period where the seller helps you learn operations.  
✅ **Key Employee Retention** – Ensure staff and management will stay post-sale.  
✅ **Supplier & Customer Relationships** – Verify contracts and key relationships are stable.
