Car dealerships operate on a financing model known as **floor planning**, where they take out loans to finance the vehicles sitting on their lots. These loans come with interest, meaning the longer a car remains unsold, the more it costs the dealership—not just in financing fees but also in insurance, maintenance, and the opportunity cost of using that space for newer inventory.

### **The Pressure to Sell Quickly**

Most dealerships aim to sell a vehicle within **60 to 90 days** of acquiring it. After that, every extra day a car sits on the lot eats into profits. Unlike retail stores that can return unsold goods to suppliers, **dealerships can’t return unsold cars to manufacturers**. So, they have a few strategies to move inventory before it becomes a financial burden:

### **1. Price Drops & Incentives**

- The most direct way to sell slow-moving cars is to **cut prices** or offer **attractive financing options**, rebates, and lease specials.
- Manufacturers may also step in with **dealer cash incentives**, allowing dealerships to discount vehicles without directly losing money.

### **2. Converting New Cars to Used**

- Some unsold new cars are converted into **Certified Pre-Owned (CPO) vehicles** by placing them into loaner or rental programs.
- If a customer’s car is in the shop for repairs, they might get a **dealership loaner car**, which then gets classified as “used” when returned.
- This allows the dealership to **reset depreciation** and potentially sell the car at a better margin while appealing to buyers looking for CPO deals.

### **3. Dealer Trades**

- If a car isn’t selling well in one market, dealerships will **trade vehicles with another dealership** where demand might be higher.
- For example, a dealership in a rural area may swap sports cars with an urban dealership for more SUVs or trucks.

### **4. Wholesale Auctions – The Last Resort**

- When all else fails, dealerships **offload aging inventory at auctions**. There are two main types:
    - **Closed Auctions** – Restricted to franchise dealers, typically offering higher-quality cars.
    - **Open Auctions** – Open to anyone, including independent used car dealers, rental companies, and even individuals.
- The biggest buyers at these auctions are **rental car companies** like Hertz and Enterprise, which purchase large fleets at discounted prices.

### **What Happens to Cars That Never Make It to a Dealership?**

- Sometimes, manufacturers overestimate demand and produce more vehicles than dealerships can take in. When this happens:
    - **Manufacturers store them** in large holding lots.
    - **Some cars are exported** to foreign markets where demand is higher.
    - **Unsellable cars may be scrapped**, particularly if they don’t meet regulatory standards in other countries.

### **How This Benefits Buyers**

- A dealership eager to move a car past its **90-day window** is often willing to **negotiate aggressively**.
- Certified Pre-Owned (CPO) vehicles, originally new cars that were used in service programs, can offer **significant discounts** while still carrying warranties.
- End-of-quarter and end-of-year sales events are prime times to buy, as dealerships push hard to clear inventory.

Understanding this process can give **buyers an edge** in negotiations and help them secure better deals on vehicles that dealerships are desperate to sell.



As a former industry insider with experience in dealership operations, financing, and car auctions, I can walk you through the **best strategies** to buy a car at the right price while avoiding common dealership tactics that cost buyers thousands.

## **1. Understand Dealer Strategy & Timing**

### **When to Buy**

- **End of the Month / Quarter / Year** → Salespeople and dealerships have quotas to hit, which makes them more likely to cut deals.
- **Holiday Sales Events** → Memorial Day, Labor Day, and Black Friday are great times when dealerships get manufacturer incentives.
- **End of a Model Year** → When the next year’s models arrive (typically fall), dealers need to clear out the older ones.
- **Rainy Days & Midweek Shopping** → Fewer customers = more attention from sales staff and a greater willingness to negotiate.

### **How Long a Car Has Been on the Lot**

- Cars that have been sitting for **60-90+ days** cost the dealership money in financing and insurance. You can check this using the **VIN on sites like CarGurus or AutoTrader**, which show days on the lot.
- The dealership will be more eager to **sell a car that’s been sitting** than a fresh arrival.

## **2. Financing Strategy – Get Pre-Approved Before You Step in the Dealership**

- **Never go into a dealership without a pre-approved loan offer from your bank or credit union.** This gives you leverage against dealer financing, which often has marked-up interest rates.
- Credit unions and online lenders (like Capital One Auto Navigator) tend to offer better rates than dealership financing.
- If the dealer offers a lower rate, **make sure it's not because they're padding the price elsewhere** (e.g., hidden fees).

## **3. Research & Avoid Dealer Markups**

- **Invoice Price vs. MSRP**: The invoice price (what the dealer pays the manufacturer) is lower than the MSRP (sticker price). Look up this info on sites like **Edmunds, Kelley Blue Book, or TrueCar**.
- **Factory Rebates & Dealer Incentives**: Sometimes, dealerships get hidden **"dealer cash" incentives** from manufacturers, meaning they can sell cars for less than invoice and still make a profit.
- **Market Adjustments & Add-Ons**: Many dealerships add junk fees like “Market Adjustment Fees” or forced add-ons (paint protection, nitrogen-filled tires, etc.). **Ask for a breakdown and refuse unnecessary charges.**

## **4. Test Drive & Psychological Tactics**

- **Don’t show too much enthusiasm**—salespeople are trained to pick up on body language. Keep your emotions neutral.
- **Test drive the car at a different dealership first** so that when you’re negotiating, you aren’t falling in love with the car in front of them.
- **Don’t negotiate based on monthly payment**—dealers will stretch out loan terms to make a bad deal look good. Always negotiate based on the **out-the-door price**.

## **5. Negotiation Strategy**

### **Start Low, Work Up Slowly**

- Open with a reasonable but **low offer** based on market research (typically $500-$2,000 below invoice, depending on demand).
- The salesperson will likely “talk to their manager” (a tactic to wear you down). Hold your ground.
- If they push back, mention **competing dealership offers** or bring up the vehicle’s time on the lot.

### **Key Negotiation Phrases**

- **“I’m ready to buy today if we can get to the right number.”** → This tells them you’re serious without giving away how much you’re willing to spend.
- **“Another dealer is offering me [X price]. Can you beat it?”** → Even if it’s not entirely true, it forces them to justify their pricing.
- **“Let me see the breakdown of fees.”** → Forces them to remove unnecessary charges.

### **Be Willing to Walk Away**

- The biggest power you have is your willingness to **walk away**. If the deal isn’t right, thank them and leave.
- Many times, the dealer will **call you back** later with a better offer.

## **6. Trade-In Strategy**

- **Get a firm offer from Carvana, CarMax, or another dealer before negotiating.** This gives you a baseline and prevents dealerships from lowballing you.
- **Negotiate your trade-in separately** from the car price to avoid hidden markups.

## **7. Final Steps: Read the Paperwork**

- **Watch for “extras”** like extended warranties, GAP insurance, or tire protection packages. These are high-margin add-ons you **don’t need** in most cases.
- **Double-check financing terms**—dealers sometimes switch loan terms last minute.

## **8. Alternative Buying Options**

### **Buying a Certified Pre-Owned (CPO) Car**

- CPO cars are **lightly used, dealer-inspected, and come with a manufacturer warranty**—a great option to avoid depreciation while still getting a reliable vehicle.
- Often, CPO vehicles were **former dealer loaner cars** that were lightly used.

### **Buying from an Auction or Direct from Rental Companies**

- **Rental car companies (Hertz, Enterprise, Avis) sell directly to the public**, often at a discount compared to dealerships.
- If you have access to **dealer-only auctions**, you can buy cars at wholesale prices, but this requires knowing someone with access.

---

### **Final Thought: Stay Patient & Be Prepared**

The best car deals come to those who **do their research, secure financing ahead of time, and aren’t afraid to walk away.** The more information you have, the harder it is for the dealership to manipulate the process in their favor.



CPO (Certified Pre-Owned) vehicles are **not promoted as aggressively** as new cars, but dealerships **heavily push them in specific situations**. Here’s why:

### **1. New Cars Get More Marketing & Incentives**

- Manufacturers prioritize selling new cars because they make more money upfront.
- New cars have **bigger advertising budgets**, with TV commercials, online ads, and dealer promotions.
- Dealers get **more incentives and bonuses** from manufacturers for selling new cars, so they push them harder.

### **2. CPO Cars Are a Backup Strategy for Dealers**

- CPO vehicles mostly come from **lease returns, trade-ins, or ex-loaner cars**.
- Dealers don’t mind selling CPOs, but they **prefer selling new cars first** because new car sales often lead to financing and warranty upsells.
- If a new car deal falls through, a salesperson might steer a buyer toward a CPO as a **"budget-friendly alternative."**

### **3. When Do Dealers Push CPOs Harder?**

- **High-Interest Rate Environments:** If financing rates for new cars are unattractive, CPOs become more appealing since they are cheaper and sometimes have better financing offers.
- **Inventory Issues:** If a dealer has too many CPO cars sitting, they might **promote them with special financing or extended warranties**.
- **Luxury Brands:** Premium brands like **Lexus, BMW, and Mercedes-Benz** push CPO hard because their vehicles depreciate quickly, and CPO helps keep resale values high.

### **4. Why CPO Doesn’t Get as Much Attention**

- **Less Manufacturer Profit:** Automakers don’t make as much money from CPO sales compared to new cars.
- **More Consumer Awareness Needed:** Shoppers who don’t research much **default to new cars** because CPO isn’t marketed as aggressively.
- **Fewer Rebates & Lease Offers:** Unlike new cars, CPO vehicles don’t come with big rebates or special lease deals, making them harder to promote in ads.

### **Final Takeaway**

CPO cars **aren’t marketed as aggressively as new cars, but dealers still like selling them—especially in certain conditions.** If you're looking for value, a CPO vehicle can be a great deal, but you often have to **seek them out rather than expect heavy advertising.**


Navigating the New York car market can be **challenging** due to high prices, taxes, and fees, but there are **strategies to get a better deal**—sometimes even by crossing state lines.

---

### **1. Buying in New York: Pros & Cons**

✅ **Pros**

- High inventory in the NYC metro area (more choices).
- Strong CPO market, especially for luxury brands.
- Dealerships may offer aggressive incentives to stay competitive.

❌ **Cons**

- Higher **taxes and fees** (NY sales tax: ~8.875% in NYC).
- **Dealer markups** can be higher due to demand.
- **Limited room for negotiation**—dealers know buyers in NYC often don’t have time to shop around.

---

### **2. Buying in New Jersey, Connecticut, or Pennsylvania**

Many New Yorkers **cross state lines** for better deals. Here’s why:

✅ **Lower Fees & Taxes**

- NJ has a **6.625% sales tax** vs. NYC’s ~8.875%.
- Some states don’t charge doc fees or have lower registration costs.

✅ **Better Negotiation Power**

- Dealers outside NYC often have **more room to negotiate** due to lower overhead costs.
- More suburban dealerships = **less competition per customer** than NYC dealers.

✅ **More Inventory in Rural/Suburban Areas**

- NJ and PA dealers may have **excess stock** that they’re more willing to discount.
- You can find **more aggressive incentives on slow-moving cars**.

❌ **Challenges of Buying Out of State**

- **You still pay NY sales tax** when registering the car in NY.
- Some dealers **refuse to sell to out-of-state buyers** due to franchise agreements.
- You may need to **handle registration and plates yourself** instead of dealer processing.

---

### **3. When Does It Make Sense to Buy Out of State?**

💡 **Best time to go out of state:**

- If buying a **high-demand car** (NY dealers might mark it up, while NJ/PA dealers might have MSRP pricing).
- If looking for a **CPO luxury car**—NYC prices are often inflated due to demand.
- If you’re willing to **drive 1-2 hours to save $1,000+ on fees and price**.

💡 **Stay in NY if:**

- You want the dealer to handle **everything (plates, registration, etc.).**
- You're financing through the dealer, and they have **exclusive incentives for NY residents**.

---

### **4. Best Strategy for New Yorkers**

- **Start your search in NY**, but don’t commit too quickly.
- **Expand your search radius** to NJ, CT, and PA to compare pricing.
- **Negotiate via email or phone first** before visiting—NYC dealers often budge more when they know you have out-of-state options.
- If going out of state, **confirm they’ll sell to a NY buyer** and check if they’ll handle registration.
- **Consider leasing in NY but buying out of state**—leasing incentives are often stronger in NY.

---

### **Bottom Line**

NYC car buying is **tough but manageable** with strategy. If you’re open to traveling, **NJ, CT, or PA can save you money**, but make sure the savings outweigh the hassle.
