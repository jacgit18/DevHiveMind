The official definition of a security in the context of financial markets is typically outlined in legal and regulatory frameworks. For example, under U.S. law, the Securities Act of 1933 defines a security as:  
  
> "Any note, stock, treasury stock, bond, debenture, evidence of indebtedness, certificate of interest or participation in any profit-sharing agreement, collateral-trust certificate, preorganization certificate or subscription, transferable share, investment contract, voting-trust certificate, certificate of deposit for a security, fractional undivided interest in oil, gas, or other mineral rights, or, in general, any interest or instrument commonly known as a 'security'."  
  
  
  
This definition includes stocks, bonds, options, derivatives, and other investment instruments that represent ownership, debt, or rights to a future benefit.  
  
Key Elements of a Security:  
  
1. Investments of Money: Investors contribute funds.  
  
  
2. Common Enterprise: The investment is pooled or tied to a larger effort, like a company or project.  
  
  
3. Expectation of Profit: Investors anticipate a financial return.  
  
  
4. Efforts of Others: The return often depends on the managerial or entrepreneurial efforts of someone other than the investor.  
  
  
  
This legal understanding was reinforced by the Howey Test (a landmark U.S. Supreme Court case), which determines whether a transaction qualifies as a security, particularly in investment contracts.  
  
In simple terms, a security is any tradable financial asset that can represent ownership (like stocks), a creditor relationship (like bonds), or a right to ownership through a contract (like options).
