The official definition of a security in financial markets is established within legal and regulatory frameworks. For instance, under U.S. law, the Securities Act of 1933 defines a security as:

> "Any note, stock, treasury stock, bond, debenture, evidence of indebtedness, certificate of interest or participation in any profit-sharing agreement, collateral-trust certificate, preorganization certificate or subscription, transferable share, investment contract, voting-trust certificate, certificate of deposit for a security, fractional undivided interest in oil, gas, or other mineral rights, or, in general, any interest or instrument commonly known as a 'security.'"

Additionally, securities include derivatives (such as options and futures) and structured products like mortgage-backed securities and collateralized debt obligations (CDOs).

### Key Characteristics of a Security:

1. **Investment of Money**: Investors provide capital or funds.
2. **Common Enterprise**: The investment is tied to a collective effort, such as a company or project.
3. **Expectation of Profit**: Investors expect financial gains or returns.
4. **Efforts of Others**: The returns typically rely on the management or entrepreneurial efforts of third parties.

This definition is further clarified by the **Howey Test**, a U.S. Supreme Court decision that outlines the criteria for determining whether a transaction constitutes a security, especially in the context of investment contracts.

### Simplified Explanation:

A security is any tradable financial asset that:

- Represents ownership (e.g., stocks),
- Denotes a creditor relationship (e.g., bonds), or
- Provides rights to future benefits through a contract (e.g., options).

In essence, securities enable individuals to invest in enterprises or financial instruments with the expectation of future returns, relying on the success of the underlying assets or ventures.
